# SLA Methods Research — Evidence-Based Pedagogy for FALA (2020–2026)

> Research for the pedagogy-upgrade effort — evidence-based second-language-acquisition (SLA) methods that apply to FALA's prompts (`prompts/system.md`, `prompts/warmup.md`). Extends `conversation-ux.md` (LLM tutor UX) and `evaluation.md` (progress measurement) without repeating them. Scope: A0→B1 European Portuguese for English speakers.

## Key findings applied to FALA

### 1. Comprehensible input (i+1): still valuable, no longer sufficient
Krashen's Input Hypothesis underwrites FALA's comprehension-first A0 method. The modern consensus keeps the core claim — massive level-appropriate input drives acquisition — but rejects the strong version. A 2025 neuro-ecological critique argues i+1 is underspecified (no way to measure "+1"), hard to falsify, and ignores interaction, embodiment, and individual differences ([Frontiers in Psychology, 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1636777/full)). Practical consequence for FALA: keep the sentence-first input loop, but treat input as one leg of a stool. The current A0 loop (hear PT → type EN) is pure input comprehension; it never requires the learner to *produce* Portuguese until "mid sessions", and the prompt gives no criterion for when that switch happens.

### 2. Retrieval practice and spaced repetition: upgrade the format and the algorithm
Retrieval practice is one of the best-replicated effects in applied psychology: a meta-analysis of 272 studies found testing beats restudy with g ≈ 0.61, and *successful* retrieval (feedback after effort) beats easy recognition ([Adesope et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306)). FALA's warm-up already does retrieval, but in the weakest format: it plays a PT sentence and asks for the EN translation — that is *recognition of meaning*, not production. Production-format retrieval (EN prompt → learner recalls the PT sentence) is the higher-yield direction and matches the "expected output" progression already sketched in system.md Rule 4.

On scheduling: FALA uses SM-2 in `progress.py` (fixed ease factor, interval reset on failure). SM-2's known failure mode is "ease hell" — repeated failures permanently depress the ease factor. FSRS replaces the ease factor with a three-component memory model (Retrievability, Stability, Difficulty) whose parameters are *fitted to the individual user's review log*, producing more accurate due dates with less workload ([Anki's algorithm FAQ](https://faqs.ankiweb.net/what-spaced-repetition-algorithm), [FSRS algorithm reference](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/wiki)). This is a code change, not a prompt change, but it directly affects which words the warm-up prompt receives.

### 3. TBLT vs. PPP: tasks beat presentation-practice-production for productive knowledge
The meta-analytic picture favors task-based language teaching: Bryfonski & McKay (2019) found large positive effects for TBLT programs across 52 studies ([LTA meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168817744389)), though later technical comments urge caution about study quality and publication bias ([Boers & Faez comment, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/13621688221131127)). Comparative work (Shintani and others) sharpens the picture: task-based lessons produce better *productive* vocabulary use than PPP lessons, while PPP-style practice only helps receptive knowledge. FALA is already closer to TBLT than PPP — it has no explicit grammar presentation — but its "free conversation" phase is unstructured chat, not a task. Giving the free-talk phase a concrete task with an outcome ("order breakfast and ask the price", "buy a metro ticket and ask about delays") converts chat into task-based interaction, which is where the evidence is strongest.

### 4. Corrective feedback: prompts over recasts — with a chat-specific caveat
The classroom meta-analysis (15 studies, N=827) found CF has significant, durable effects, that **prompts** (elicitation, clarification requests, metalinguistic clues — which push the learner to self-repair) outperform **recasts** (the tutor reformulates silently), and that effects are largest on measures requiring free constructed responses ([Lyster & Saito, 2010](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ892626)). This partially conflicts with the current system.md rule "never reveal the correct answer, guide with hints": the hints rule is right in spirit (prompts > recasts), but the "gentle in-flow correction" example in system.md is a recast, and recasts are the *weaker* option for development. In LLM chat specifically, a 2026 RCT (66 learners, one semester) found immediate vs. end-of-conversation CF produced equal learning gains, but immediate feedback scored much better on user experience ([Kamel, 2026, Frontiers in Education](https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2026.1703664)). So: correct immediately, but prefer prompts/elicitation over recasts for errors the learner can plausibly fix, and reserve recasts for input-comprehension moments where a production demand would stall the conversation.

### 5. Output and interaction: production is a learning event, not just a test
Swain's Output Hypothesis holds that producing language forces learners to notice gaps, test hypotheses, and process syntax that comprehension alone skips; the meta-analytic follow-up (Keck et al., 2006) found output tasks with reformulation opportunities outperform comprehension-only conditions. The Interaction Hypothesis (Long, 1996) adds that negotiation of meaning during interaction makes input comprehensible at the right moment. A 2025 meta-synthesis of AI-chatbot studies confirms chatbots can host this negotiation, and reports reduced learner anxiety as a consistent benefit — but also that rigorous learning-gain evidence is still scarce ([ReCALL meta-synthesis, 2010–2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/use-of-artificially-intelligent-chatbots-in-english-language-learning-a-systematic-metasynthesis-study-of-articles-published-between-2010-and-2024/118654390007CCA33DEAD142D45C8D4F)). A caution from the task-interaction meta-analysis: the empirical link between specific interactional moves and acquisition is weaker than theory predicts ([Jackson & Suethanapornkul, 2013](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/206181/)). For FALA: every session should contain at least one learner-*produced* PT utterance with an opportunity to repair it, from early sessions onward — even a two-word production counts.

### 6. Processing instruction: structured input as a better comprehension drill
Processing Instruction (VanPatten) replaces mechanical drills with **structured input activities**: interpretation tasks engineered so the learner must process the target form to get the meaning (e.g., choosing the right picture forces attention on word order or clitic placement). The original studies (VanPatten & Cadierno, 1993, Spanish) and replications found PI beats traditional output drills on both interpretation and production, with gains persisting at delayed post-tests ([VanPatten & Cadierno replication material](https://pure.port.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/6107534/Input_manipulation_enhancement_and_processing.pdf)). FALA's "no grammar explanations" rule is compatible with PI — PI never explains rules, it restructures input. The current warm-up ("what did I say?" → translation) always tests whole-sentence comprehension; a PI variant would sometimes force the learner to process a *specific form* (e.g., two nearly identical sentences distinguished only by "o/a" gender agreement, or clitic position).

### 7. LLM-tutor studies (2024–2026): which prompt-level behaviors track gains
Direct evidence is young but converging on a few behaviors:
- **Immediate, in-flow correction is fine.** The timing RCT found no gain penalty for immediate CF and a clear UX win ([Kamel, 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2026.1703664)) — FALA's in-flow approach is validated.
- **Structured prompt patterns beat naive ones.** In L2 Portuguese writing, untrained learner prompting produced misaligned, error-prone revisions, while a simple "rephrase-and-respond" pattern elicited the most accurate revisions; forcing justification increased turns but also error rates ([You & Zhang, 2025, Texto Livre](https://periodicos.ufmg.br/index.php/textolivre/article/view/63188)). Lesson for FALA: give the tutor a fixed correction *procedure* (reproduce the learner's intent → rephrase correctly → one focused prompt), not a vague "be helpful".
- **Pedagogical guardrails in the prompt matter.** The 8-dimension taxonomy and teacher-authored-prompt findings already applied in `conversation-ux.md` (no answer-revealing, actionability, encouraging tone) remain the strongest prompt-level correlates in this literature.
- **Affective factors are real but secondary.** Chatbot studies consistently report reduced speaking anxiety; useful for FALA's voice mode, but not a substitute for gains evidence ([ReCALL meta-synthesis](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/use-of-artificially-intelligent-chatbots-in-english-language-learning-a-systematic-metasynthesis-study-of-articles-published-between-2010-and-2024/118654390007CCA33DEAD142D45C8D4F)).

### 8. European Portuguese specifics: CAPLE alignment and pt-PT form trouble spots
The official exam framework is CAPLE-ULisboa: **CIPLE = A2, DEPLE = B1** (DIPLE is B2, not B1 — a common mislabel) ([CAPLE exams](https://caple.letras.ulisboa.pt/exames/)). CAPLE tasks are integrated, real-life comprehension and interaction tasks — ordering, transactions, short exchanges — which matches FALA's scenario list almost exactly. Two pt-PT-specific trouble spots appear repeatedly in PLE research: **clitic pronoun placement** (proclisis vs. ênclise — "vejo-o" vs. "o vejo"; enclisis after subject or comma) is the best-documented persistent learner error ([PLE clitic-placement study](https://rodin.uca.es/bitstream/handle/10498/31936/Env%C3%ADo%2BTelma%2BDuarte.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y)), and the exam's reading component stresses real-life text processing over grammar items ([CIPLE component analysis, ULisboa](https://researchportal.ulisboa.pt/files/108146563/ulflazhang_tm.pdf)). FALA's system prompt already guards Brazilianisms (você, gerund) but says nothing about clitic placement — the single most pt-PT-specific form a B1-bound learner must process correctly.

## Recommendations for FALA prompts

Ranked. Each item names the finding it implements and a check an eval harness can run on generated transcripts.

1. **Replace "never reveal the answer" with a two-track feedback rule.** For learner *production* errors: prompt first (elicitation or a metalinguistic clue, e.g., "Almost — which little word goes *before* the verb here?"), and reveal only after two failed attempts. For *comprehension* moments: keep the recast-style model ("Sim! [sentence] — that means [translation]"). Finding: prompts > recasts for development (Lyster & Saito, 2010); immediate CF is safe (Kamel, 2026). *Testable:* judge labels each correction turn as prompt / recast / reveal; harness measures uptake rate (learner reformulates correctly within 2 turns) and asserts prompts ≥ recasts for production errors.

2. **Require at least one learner-produced PT utterance per session, from session 1.** Add to the A0 loop: after each sentence is comprehended twice, ask the learner to produce a fragment ("Now you: ask for the soup — try it, two words is fine"). Accept any attempt; model the full correct version after. Finding: output with reformulation drives gains beyond comprehension (Swain; Keck et al., 2006). *Testable:* count turns where the learner's message contains PT words; assert ≥1 per session and that the tutor models the corrected version within the next turn.

3. **Convert warm-up retrieval to production format for known words.** In `prompts/warmup.md`, for words with confidence ≥ 0.5, give the EN and ask the learner to recall the PT ("How would you ask for soup?"), instead of playing PT and asking for EN. Keep PT→EN for new or low-confidence words. Finding: successful retrieval beats recognition (Adesope et al., 2017). *Testable:* harness classifies warm-up review items as recall-direction (EN→PT) vs. recognition-direction (PT→EN); assert recall-direction share rises with word confidence.

4. **Give the free-conversation phase a concrete task.** Change the phase transition to open a small task with a checkable outcome ("You're at a pastelaria: order a coffee and ask the price") instead of "tell me about [topic]". Rotate tasks from the existing scenario list. Finding: TBLT outperforms unstructured/PPP formats for productive use (Bryfonski & McKay, 2019; Shintani). *Testable:* rubric item — "did the free-talk phase have a stated goal and an outcome moment?"; judge scores 0/1 per session.

5. **Add one structured-input moment per session targeting a pt-PT form.** For A1+ learners, present two minimal-pair sentences that differ only in the target form (clitic position, gender agreement, ser/estar) and ask the learner to pick which matches a picture/meaning — no grammar explanation. Prioritize clitic placement, the documented pt-PT learner bottleneck. Finding: processing instruction builds form–meaning mapping without rule-teaching (VanPatten & Cadierno); clitics are the top pt-PT error (Duarte). *Testable:* judge detects minimal-pair interpretation exchanges; assert ≥1 per session at A1+, and that the tutor never appends a grammar-rule explanation.

6. **Fix the CF procedure in the prompt: restate → rephrase → one focus.** Spell out the correction sequence: (a) restate what the learner meant, (b) give the correct PT version naturally, (c) end with at most one prompt question on the single highest-value error. Finding: structured prompt patterns produce the most accurate corrections; multi-issue justification-style feedback raises error rates in LLM output (You & Zhang, 2025). *Testable:* judge counts corrections per tutor turn (assert ≤1 focus question) and checks the correct PT model is present.

7. **Anchor level progression to CAPLE descriptors.** In the level guidance, define A2 exit as CIPLE-style can-dos (handle café/transport/shopping transactions) and B1 as DEPLE-style (handle unpredictable everyday situations, give simple accounts). Findings: CAPLE defines CIPLE=A2, DEPLE=B1, and its tasks are transactional ([CAPLE](https://caple.letras.ulisboa.pt/exames/)). *Testable:* rubric maps each session's scenario to a CAPLE can-do; harness flags sessions with no can-do coverage.
